# Style profile

## Scope and priority

Target the classic Monet Impressionist visual language associated with the 1870s: direct observation, transient natural light, colored shadows, broken directional strokes, optical color mixing, open-air atmosphere, and an informal but controlled composition. Do not teach or emphasize a chronology of Monet's stylistic development. A work from another date may contribute a reference crop only when that crop clearly serves this classic visual language. Bright, luminous water-lily references are allowed when they obey the same rules. Exclude dark or blue-dominant late-water-lily treatment and near-abstraction unless the user explicitly overrides that boundary.

The main subject is landscape. Figures may appear as secondary or scene-defining elements, and the dedicated `figures` references should be used when pose, clothing, face simplification, or figure–landscape integration matters.

## Central perceptual aim

Do not merely repaint what an object is. Reconstruct how the scene appears in one passing condition of light, air, weather, moisture, wind, steam, reflection, or movement. Name that condition before describing the brushwork. Objects are the carriers of the light event, but they must remain recognizable enough to anchor the scene.

## Translating photographic light into brushwork

A photograph and a classic Impressionist painting can describe the same observed light event, but they encode it differently. The photograph records continuous luminance, color, focus, reflection, and atmospheric falloff. The painting must translate those signals into visible, separated color decisions.

Before restyling a photograph, identify:

1. the dominant light direction and time/weather character;
2. the largest highlight, midtone, and shadow families;
3. the warm/cool relationship between direct light, ambient light, and reflected light;
4. haze, moisture, glare, reflection, wind, or motion cues;
5. which edges are structural anchors and which are weakened by light or atmosphere.

Translate them as follows:

- **Direct light:** use grouped high-value chromatic strokes, not featureless white fill.
- **Colored shadow:** replace neutral darkening with cooler or reflected environmental hues while preserving readable value separation.
- **Glare and sparkle:** break highlights into intermittent marks whose direction follows water, leaves, snow, cloth, or masonry.
- **Atmospheric distance:** reduce contrast and saturation, compress values, and use smaller or quieter marks; do not apply uniform blur.
- **Edge behavior:** keep a few high-information contours firm, but let glare, mist, backlight, steam, or reflection interrupt other edges through neighboring color strokes.
- **Wind and movement:** align stroke direction and rhythm with moving grass, cloth, cloud, water, smoke, or steam instead of adding photographic motion blur.
- **Local color:** preserve what the object is only after accounting for the color cast of the observed light.

The photograph remains authoritative for subject, composition, perspective, and the actual lighting event. The Impressionist transformation changes the representation of that light, not the event itself. Avoid replacing the source with a generic golden-hour, foggy, or pastel preset.

## Non-negotiable visual traits

### Discrete, juxtaposed strokes

- Make the stroke or small color mark visible rather than polishing it into a seamless surface.
- Keep selected adjacent marks distinct; other edges can drag, merge or disappear inside a coherent larger color mass.
- Build local color through warm/cool, complementary, and light/dark adjacency.
- Use optical mixing: colors resolve at viewing distance but remain separate up close.
- Avoid smudging every transition into a single intermediate color. Broad low-contrast passages are nevertheless valid and need not be filled with separately outlined dabs.
- Concentrate the strongest adjacency at actual light, material, or form transitions; allow quieter related-color passages elsewhere. Do not maximize contrast around every stroke.

This does not require identical marks everywhere. Water, foliage, cloud, masonry, clothing, and skin should have different stroke rhythms.

### Open but controlled paint handling

- Suggest rapid outdoor observation through short, broken, directional marks, but do not imitate careless scribbling.
- Let comparatively solid, matte-looking painted passages carry the scene; vary them with local thin stain, dragged paint, denser opaque marks, and selective impasto rather than applying one raised texture everywhere.
- Make the *visual* paint load uneven: some passages cover what is below, some skim and break, and some softly meet a neighbor. Varied length, width, pressure-like taper, and interrupted edges matter more than an added 3D texture filter.
- Avoid long runs of similarly wide, similarly smooth, translucent color ribbons. They read as marker or digital overlay even when the hues are juxtaposed correctly.
- Allow occasional light ground or canvas-like breathing space where it helps luminosity; do not fake random bare patches across the image.
- Permit controlled wet-into-wet interaction in mist, steam, sky, or immediate transitions, alongside wet-over-dry accents and revisions.
- Keep the user's core rule intact: soft interaction may occur locally, but adjacent color identities must not collapse into globally smooth digital blending.

### Light before local color

- Organize color around the observed light, weather, time of day, and atmospheric distance.
- Permit a familiar object to shift hue when reflected light or atmosphere demands it.
- Keep luminous highlights chromatic rather than relying on flat white.
- Keep shadows responsive to environmental color while retaining their relative darkness and area. Muted grays, browns and near-black structural accents are valid when observed; colored shadow does not mean uniformly bright violet.

### Varied directional rhythm

- Water: broken horizontal or gently rippling marks, with vertical reflection cues where needed.
- Foliage and grass: clustered, irregular touches that follow mass and growth without outlining every leaf.
- Sky and cloud: broader, airier marks; allow limited soft transition in distant haze while keeping chromatic variation.
- Buildings and bridges: retain readable geometry, then interrupt hard contours with light and atmospheric color.
- Figures: preserve gesture, pose, silhouette, and identity-bearing contrast; simplify small details into economical color marks.

### Recognition and structure

The result must still read as the original or requested scene. Preserve major silhouettes, horizon placement, perspective, spatial divisions, and the relationship between dominant objects. Impressionist simplification may remove small detail but must not dissolve the scene into a texture field.

### Instantaneous, informal composition

- Prefer a scene that feels selected from continuous life rather than arranged as a formal stage.
- Use asymmetry, cropped edges, oblique movement, or a high/low viewpoint when these strengthen the observed moment.
- Flatten space selectively through horizontal color bands or surface pattern while retaining enough overlap, scale, and value structure for orientation.
- Let a bridge, horizon, road, figure diagonal, architectural frame, or dark silhouette stabilize otherwise dissolving atmosphere.
- Avoid random Dutch angles, arbitrary cut-off subjects, or lens-like distortion with no observational purpose.

### Landscape and modern life

The style is not limited to gardens and untouched countryside. Ports, bridges, railways, stations, steam, smoke, industrial rivers, suburban walks, seaside leisure, modern clothing, and moving boats are valid subjects. For modern-life scenes, retain a readable geometric or directional framework, then let air, steam, light, and movement partially interrupt it.

### Figures inside the environment

Figures usually serve the light, movement, scale, and composition rather than behaving as isolated studio portraits. Preserve a person's pose, silhouette, gesture, and identity-bearing contrasts. Simplify facial detail in proportion to distance, and let clothing pick up sky, grass, snow, or reflected light without dissolving the person.

## Style strength

- **Light:** approximately 25. Mostly preserve the source palette and modeling; add broken strokes and modest chromatic separation.
- **Balanced:** approximately 55. Preserve composition and recognition; actively reinterpret palette, illumination, edge hierarchy, and mark-making.
- **Strong:** approximately 80. Repaint color and atmosphere assertively, enlarge and separate marks, simplify secondary detail, and soften boundaries between neighboring objects so the scene feels more unified, while retaining major forms.

At **strong or above (67–100)**, progressively soften more object-to-object edges with overlapping strokes, close neighboring colors, scumbling, and atmospheric interruption. Let some boundaries dissolve into shared light or air so the whole scene feels integrated. Keep a few high-information edges and silhouettes clear enough to identify objects, preserve depth and the source composition, and avoid a uniform digital blur or soft-focus filter.

Map custom 0–100 values continuously between these anchors. A value above 80 increases chromatic and stroke emphasis plus selective edge fusion, not abstraction or subject drift.

## Bright water-lily rule

Prefer daylight, pearl, warm green, yellow-green, soft rose, lilac, turquoise, and reflected sky notes where the source light supports them. Maintain readable lily pads, blossoms, water plane, or garden structure. Brightness is relational: preserve the source's shadowed pads and dark water gaps, and concentrate highlights where they are observed instead of whitening every leaf. Do not let navy, indigo, black-blue, or invented dusk-like darkness dominate the image. Avoid the heavy, nearly nonrepresentational treatment associated with some late panels.

## Failure conditions

Reject or revise outputs showing any of the following:

- uniform “oil-paint” filter over photographic detail;
- smooth gradients across most of the image;
- muddy intermediate browns or grays caused by overmixing;
- soft-focus haze with no discrete color marks;
- impasto-like 3D texture applied indiscriminately;
- hard black outlines around every object;
- repetitive digital dabs of identical size and direction;
- a superficially unfinished or chaotic look with no compositional anchor;
- cinematic depth of field, camera bokeh, or photographic motion blur standing in for painted atmosphere;
- arbitrary angled framing or crop that damages subject recognition;
- neon saturation unrelated to light;
- translucent highlighter-like color bands, uniform swatch-shaped strokes, or equally crisp stroke borders across large areas;
- strong warm/cool contrast spread so evenly that no quiet middle tones remain;
- simulated raised canvas or impasto filter used to mask flat digital strokes;
- subject, face, architecture, or composition drifting beyond recognition;
- dark, blue-dominant water-lily treatment by default.
